NATO Told 32 Countries to PREPARE. Here's What For

Presented by Damyan Ivanov · Every claim checked

The briefing

NATO and the Pentagon are preparing for a grid attack, not an invasion. Here's what the documents reveal, and what it means for your household.

In September 2026, war preparation in the West went public. NATO published its civil resilience requirements for the first time: keep the government running, keep energy and communications up, and stockpile blood and medicine. Two weeks later, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth launched FORTRESS, a plan to keep US military bases powered "if an adversary takes down the grid." According to Fox News Digital, internal documents set a 90-day target for every major base. Meanwhile Britain's "Be Ready" campaign, the European Union and Germany asked families to prepare for 3 to 10 days. In this video we read the documents themselves: what NATO and the Pentagon expect to be hit, who they are planning against, and why officials insist this is not a war warning. Every claim is checked and sourced on our website.

Section by section

Why governments are telling people to prepare: Sweden's leaflet to 5.2 million households (Nov 2024), Germany's advice to stock up for up to 10 days, Britain's three days and the European Union's 72 hours.

FORTRESS, the Pentagon's 90-day plan: Federal Operations for Resilience, Troop Resources, Energy Supply and Survivability, launched by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth at Quantico, Virginia (30 Sep 2026). Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell: "Power is combat now." Reserves of munitions, fuel, critical minerals, electronics, spare parts and medical supplies; up to $2.2 billion for microreactors at 5 Army bases; about 500 bases assessed. The Belfer Center at Harvard Kennedy School found bases rely "almost exclusively on the civilian electric grid."

NATO's seven requirements: the Baseline Requirements for Resilience published on 17 Sep 2026 ("Prepare, protect, prevail"), the Hague Summit pledge of up to 1.5% of GDP for resilience and civil preparedness, and Secretary General Mark Rutte's "resist, respond to and quickly recover."

Who they're planning against: Russia, and the hybrid attacks NATO says now hit "infrastructure and digital networks" more often; a NATO official's "no imminent threat of attack" (Reuters, 30 Aug 2026); and UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting's "a little over 1,000 days" warning.

What families are asked to do: Britain's Be Ready checklist (water, food that needs no cooking, a flashlight, a radio, a power bank, cash), about 3 liters of water per person per day, bottled water sales up 67% in about ten days, FEMA's 72 hours in the United States, and Vice President JD Vance on Fox News.

Bases get 90 days, families get 3, and three scenarios: preparation works as deterrence, the gap stays open, or fatigue sets in.

Three scenarios: preparation works as deterrence, because a base that keeps running and a city that rides out the first days make cutting the grid less worth it; the gap stays open, because FORTRESS could take decades and household campaigns depend on families who can afford to stock up, so the weak point becomes the town around the base; or fatigue, where every new checklist lands as background noise and people stop listening before the moment it matters. Britain's own minister asked people to be "alert but not alarmed."

Full transcript

0:00 Why governments are telling people to prepare

Look at this map. Every light is a government telling its people to get ready. So what exactly are they getting ready for? Sweden mailed a crisis booklet to every household.

Germany says keep up to ten days of supplies. Britain says three days. The European Union says seventy-two hours. Then the last light comes on.

It's America. And there, the plan isn't for families. It's for military bases. Fox News Digital says it saw internal Pentagon planning documents.

According to that report, every major base in the continental U.S. would be expected to run for at least ninety days. On its own. No outside power. No outside water.

No outside fuel. Here's why that number matters. Those bases run almost entirely on the civilian grid. The same grid your house runs on.

This is the pivot. For years, war preparation lived in closed plans. Then, in two weeks of September 2026, it went public. NATO published its resilience requirements for the first time.

Then the Pentagon launched a plan to keep its bases running through a grid attack. From that point, the question isn't whether they're preparing. It's what for. So, three questions.

What does the Pentagon's plan reveal? What does NATO's list reveal? And what does all of this mean for your house?

1:30 Five dates: how fast this moved

First, how fast this moved. Five dates. November 2024. Sweden mails its updated booklet, I quote "If Crisis or War Comes." It goes to five point two million households.

October 2025. Germany's civil protection office publishes its first guide that plans for war. It advises supplies for up to ten days. September 2026.

NATO goes public. Two weeks later, the Pentagon launches FORTRESS. October twenty-second, 2026. Britain launches a campaign called "Be Ready." November third, 2026.

The European Union asks every household to plan for seventy-two hours. Two years. From one country's booklet to a whole alliance's checklist.

2:21 FORTRESS: the Pentagon's 90-day plan

But the most revealing document isn't for families at all. September thirtieth, 2026. Quantico, Virginia. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth speaks to the troops.

He names a new program. FORTRESS. His promise was blunt. "If an adversary takes down the grid by cyberattack, by sabotage, or by any means, our bases stay lit." Then he added two more lines.

"Our aircraft keep flying. Our command centers never go dark." Listen to the first sentence again. again. It doesn't say a storm. It says an adversary.

The Pentagon's chief spokesman, Sean Parnell, needed four words. "Power is combat now." And he named the problem. "We don't want to rely on standard civilian municipal infrastructure to run our national defense." Then came the ninety days. Fox News Digital says internal documents set that target for every major base.

Electricity, water, fuel and communications. All of it, from the base itself. And it goes beyond power. According to Fortune, FORTRESS calls for strategic reserves.

Munitions, fuel and critical minerals. Electronics, spare parts and medical supplies. For the most critical items, it wants at least two ways to get them. That's a plan for supply lines that get cut.

Some of the hardware is already moving. In August, the Army announced up to two point two billion dollars for nuclear microreactors. They'd go to five bases, including Fort Bragg and Fort Hood. That's according to Fortune.

There is a catch, and it's important. The Pentagon hasn't publicly confirmed the ninety-day figure. According to Fortune, a senior defense official gave the scale. About five hundred bases are being assessed.

Full implementation could take decades. So what does a ninety-day target tell us? Here's my read. Storm planning usually counts in days.

Ninety days is a number for something longer. A fight where someone breaks the grid faster than crews can fix it. If you want the documents, not the panic, that's what we do here. Subscribing helps us keep reading them for you.

So the Pentagon is planning for a long fight.

4:47 NATO's seven requirements

What is NATO telling its governments? September seventeenth, 2026. NATO publishes its Baseline Requirements for Resilience. For years, these lived inside governments.

Now anyone can read them. There are seven. And the list reads like a plan for a country under pressure. Keep the government running.

Keep the energy flowing. Manage the movement of people, including what NATO calls a "mass influx." Keep food and water going through a "sustained" crisis. Handle mass casualties, with stockpiles of blood and medicine. Keep transport moving.

Keep communications up. NATO has thirty-two members. Every one of them can now be measured against this list. And there's money behind it.

At the 2025 summit in The Hague, allies agreed on a new spending rule. They can count up to 1.5% of GDP toward security spending. That includes civil preparedness. Now look at the reason NATO gives.

It points to hybrid attacks "on our societies, infrastructure and digital networks." And it says they "occur more frequently." Hybrid attacks mean sabotage, cyberattacks and pressure that stops short of open war. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte put the goal simply. Allies must be able to "resist, respond to and quickly recover." So what does the list tell us? It tells us what planners expect to be hit.

Not tanks at a border. Power, water, hospitals and phone lines. Which raises the obvious question. Who is NATO planning against, and how soon?

6:28 Who they're planning against

NATO's own answer is careful. On August thirtieth, 2026, a NATO official spoke to Reuters. The official said Russia is stepping up hybrid acts in Europe. In the same breath, the official said the alliance sees "no imminent threat of attack." Hold on to both halves.

More sabotage. No invasion in sight. Britain's Defence Secretary went further, on a longer clock. Wes Streeting wrote in The Telegraph about Russia.

By 2030, he argued, its forces and war economy could put it in a position to attack. That leaves, he wrote, "a little over 1,000 days" to prepare. That's a planning deadline, not a prediction. And he gave the logic in one line.

"To prevent the worst, we have to prepare for it." Here's the fair reading. Nobody in these governments says war is coming. They say attacks on daily life are already happening, in small pieces. And a bigger attack is less tempting if it doesn't work.

But if the bases get ninety days, how long do families get?

7:38 What families are asked to do

October twenty-second, 2026. Britain launches "Be Ready." It's the first stage of a three-year campaign. The ask is three days without power, water, phone or internet. Bottled water.

Food that needs no cooking. A flashlight, a radio and a power bank. And cash, in case card payments fail. Water at about three liters a day, per person.

People listened. According to Worldpanel data reported by Al Jazeera, bottled water sales jumped sixty-seven percent. That was in roughly the first ten days. Canned food was up thirty-seven percent.

Not everyone can afford to. An open letter cited by Al Jazeera puts a number on it. It says twenty-nine percent of Britons can't afford a three-day food reserve. Streeting's own phrase for the mood was "alert but not alarmed." On November third, the European Commission asked every EU household to manage seventy-two hours alone.

I live in Bulgaria, on NATO's Black Sea region on NATO's flank. So that guidance covers my home too. Part of the idea came from Finland. Its program is literally called "seventy-two hours." Commissioner Hadja Lahbib said "Being prepared does not have to be a expensive." And in America?

On November sixteenth, Vice President JD Vance was asked on Fox News about stockpiling. "My answer to that is no," he said. "We don't see a crisis coming down. But you have to be prepared for everything." He framed it as ordinary planning.

"That doesn't mean crisis is around the corner," he said. "That just means you've got a government who's actually preparing for it." FEMA's standing advice is supplies for at least seventy-two hours.

9:31 Bases get 90 days, families get 3

So line them up. Military bases: ninety days. Families: about three. That gap is the real story.

Planners are hardening the military first. Civilians are being asked to cover the first few days themselves. And the people asked to cover those days aren't all equally able to.

9:51 Three scenarios

So where does this go from here? Three ways this can play out. This is analysis, not a forecast. One.

Preparation works as deterrence. Cutting the grid stops being worth it. A base keeps running. A city rides out the first days.

Sabotage loses its value. That's the outcome NATO is paying for. Two. The gap stays open.

FORTRESS could take decades. Household campaigns depend on families who can afford to stock up. Then the weak point isn't the base. It's the town around it.

The soldiers have power. Their families, just outside the gate, may not. Three. Fatigue.

Every new checklist lands as background noise. People stop listening before the moment it matters. Britain's own minister asked for "alert but not alarmed." Fatigue is what happens when you get neither. Which one wins depends less on Moscow than on budgets and attention in the West.

10:52 My verdict

My verdict: this isn't a war warning. It's an admission about where the next fight could start. Not at the border. At the power plant.

The plans now cover bases for ninety days, and families for about three. So here's my question. Should families cover those first three days, or is that the government's job? Tell me in the comments.
