The Middle East's Energy Crisis Just Got Much WORSE

Presented by Damyan Ivanov · Every claim checked

The briefing

Iran's war has reached the world's second oil gate. Hormuz traffic collapsed, the Saudi bypass is under fire, and Bab el-Mandeb is slipping.

Oil through the Strait of Hormuz fell from 21.6 million barrels a day to 4.9 million, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. The world's backup was Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline to Yanbu on the Red Sea. In mid-November 2026, that backup moved inside the war: the Houthis claimed drone strikes on its pumping stations, and Yemen's government pulled back from the coast at Bab el-Mandeb, the Red Sea's southern gate. In this video we look at who really controls Hormuz (Iran's terms against CENTCOM's escorts), what the attacks on the pipeline actually hit, why Yemen's war now threatens the second gate, and what it is costing you: Brent in the nineties, record diesel, and shipping that pays twice. Then three scenarios: a deal, a second gate falling, or a long stalemate. Every claim is checked, and every claim is labelled as confirmed or claimed.

Section by section

Hormuz lost most of its oil: flows through the Strait of Hormuz fell from 21.6 to 4.9 million barrels a day (EIA, Q4 2025 to Q2 2026), with Bab el-Mandeb and Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline as the next links in the chain.

Who controls Hormuz? Iran's terms for shipping ("designated safe zones," coordination with its navy) against U.S. Central Command (CENTCOM) under Adm. Brad Cooper; at least $25 billion in Iranian revenue cut off by the U.S. blockade; UKMTO reports of tankers hit near Khasab, Oman; an IRGC claim about a radar in Bahrain that CENTCOM called "FALSE"; and Qeshm Island inside the strait.

The way around: the East-West pipeline, about 750 miles (1,200 km) from Saudi Arabia's Gulf coast to Yanbu on the Red Sea, with 11 pumping stations and 7 million barrels a day of capacity. Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Energy reported a fire but no disruption (9 Nov 2026); the Houthis claimed hits on Pumping Stations 8 and 9 (17 Nov 2026, not confirmed).

The second gate: Bab el-Mandeb, where flows rose from 5.4 to 8.1 million barrels a day, and Yemen's government pulled back as the Houthis advanced to about 62 miles (100 km) west of Aden. With analyst Mohammed Al-Qadhi (to Reuters) and Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree.

What it costs you: Brent from about $72 before the war to $95.34 (16 Nov 2026), record U.S. diesel above $6 a gallon and regular gas near $4.10 (AAA), and shipping costs that hit twice.

Can a deal reopen Hormuz? Iran's offer, with Qatar mediating, to reopen the strait in two days if the U.S. accepts "seven core conditions," and Donald Trump's answer on Truth Social. Then three scenarios: a deal, the second gate falling, or a long stalemate.

Three scenarios: a deal reopens Hormuz, with Iran's two-day offer as the starting point, and prices fall fastest; the second gate falls further, as the Houthis keep hitting pumping stations and both exits from the Gulf's oil system are contested at once, the scenario markets have not priced; or a long stalemate, where Washington keeps the blockade and escorts, Iran keeps its terms, and the pipeline keeps getting hit and repaired.
