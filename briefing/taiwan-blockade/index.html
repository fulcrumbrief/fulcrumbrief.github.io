China’s Blockade Strategy: Why It’s More DANGEROUS Than You Think

Presented by Damyan Ivanov · Every claim checked

The briefing

If China blockades Taiwan, it may not start with an invasion. Here are the first seven days, from published CSIS war games.

Everyone pictures landing craft and beaches. But in December 2025, China's "Justice Mission" drills simulated a blockade of Taiwan's major ports. Washington's CSIS think tank has modeled how it could start: a coast guard "quarantine" with customs rules, boardings and paperwork, which CSIS says "is not a declaration of war against Taiwan." Every step is a scenario based on published studies, not a prediction. Every claim is checked and sourced on our website.

Section by section

What Beijing actually rehearses: in December 2025, the People's Liberation Army's Eastern Theater Command ran the "Justice Mission" drills, simulating a blockade of Taiwan's major ports. The China Coast Guard has more than 150 oceangoing ships; Taiwan's Coast Guard Administration has about 10 (CSIS, 2024). Taiwan keeps about 11 days of natural gas in storage.

How Beijing has practiced: from Nancy Pelosi's August 2022 visit to Taipei and the drills that followed, to China's Maritime Safety Administration announcing "on-site inspections" in the Taiwan Strait (April 2023), the China Coast Guard boarding a Taiwanese tourist boat near Kinmen (19 February 2024), the "Strait Thunder" drills (April 2025) and 89 PLA aircraft counted by Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense in a single day (29 December 2025).

The scenario, day by day (built from two Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) studies, 2024 and 2025): Day 1: Beijing declares "enhanced customs inspection rules", effective in 48 hours. A quarantine run by the coast guard, not a military blockade. Days 2–3: boardings begin, first around the port of Kaohsiung, then possibly a 24-nautical-mile (about 28-mile) zone around the whole island. CSIS: compliance by around 75 percent of shipping companies "would be a considerable success for China." Day 4: Washington's problem: white-hulled coast guard ships against gray-hulled U.S. Navy warships. In a CSIS survey, only 13% of U.S. experts and 9% of Taiwan experts were "completely confident" the U.S. would intervene militarily. Day 5: Taiwan's energy clock, from its Ministry of Economic Affairs: natural gas about 10–11 days, coal about 39, oil about 146. In the CSIS "Lights Out?" wargame, electricity falls to about 20% within two months without preparation, and holds at about 80% with it. Day 6: Japan, which CSIS calls "critical": the Nansei (southwest) islands as a cargo route, and the U.S. bases Japan hosts. Day 7: who fires first? CSIS co-author Eric Heginbotham on why a blockade "puts the onus of firing the first shot onto the Taiwanese", the counterpoint from another expert at the launch, and retired U.S. Navy Admiral Scott Swift on convoys.

If someone fires: the CSIS blockade wargames (more than 20 games of 20 weeks each, led by Mark Cancian) and the 2023 CSIS invasion study "The First Battle of the Next War" (24 rounds).

What it means for you: Taiwan makes about 9 in 10 of the world's most advanced chips (EE Times Asia puts it at 92% for the smallest nodes), the chips in phones, cars and AI servers. For Japan, South Korea and Australia it is the shipping lanes next door; for an American family, the price of anything with a chip inside.

Three ways it ends, and the verdict: the quiet squeeze works; Taiwan and its partners run the blockade; or an off-ramp.

0:00 What Beijing actually rehearses

Everyone pictures the same thing. Landing craft. Beaches. An invasion of Taiwan.

But look at what Beijing actually rehearses. In December 2025, China's military drilled a blockade of Taiwan's major ports. So what happens on the day China stops ships instead of storming beaches? And who blinks first?

The first ships to arrive might not even be warships. They could be coast guard. China's coast guard has more than 150 ocean going ships. Taiwan's has about ten.

Count the smaller boats too, and it's about four hundred against about a hundred sixty. And China's coast guard isn't a normal police force. It reports to China's top military authority. And Taiwan has a clock.

It keeps about eleven days of natural gas in storage. This is the pivot of the whole scenario. A blockade like this doesn't begin with a battle. It begins with paperwork.

It becomes a war at one moment. The first shot. And the studies say neither side wants to fire it. So, three questions.

How would it start? Who would have to fire first? And what does it mean for you?

1:18 How Beijing has practiced

First, how Beijing has been practicing. August 2022. Nancy Pelosi visits Taipei. China answers with major drills around the island.

It's the first of several big exercises aimed at Taiwan. April 2023. Taiwan's president stops over in the U.S. China's maritime agency then announces "on-site inspections" in the Strait.

No ship is boarded. But the idea is now on the record. February 2024. Near the island of Kinmen, Chinese coast guard officers board a Taiwanese tourist boat.

They check its licenses and paperwork. June 2024. The CSIS think tank in Washington publishes a study. It's called "How China Could Quarantine Taiwan." April 2025.

China runs its "Strait Thunder" drills. July 2025. CSIS publishes a second study. It wargames a full blockade.

December 2025. China's "Justice Mission" drills simulate a blockade of Taiwan's major ports. Taiwan counts eighty-nine military aircraft in a single day. Navy and coast guard ships sail together, twenty-eight of them.

The drills follow a record U.S. arms package for Taiwan. China's military calls them "a stern warning" against "external interference forces." Three years. From boarding a tourist boat to rehearsing a siege. So how would the real thing unfold?

Let's walk it, day by day.

3:01 The scenario, day by day

Everything from here is a scenario. It's built from those two CSIS studies. It is not a prediction.

3:08 Day 1: an announcement

Day one. No missiles. An announcement. In the CSIS model, Beijing declares "enhanced customs inspection rules." Ships bound for Taiwan must file declarations in advance.

The rules take effect forty-eight hours later. That gives ships at sea time to comply. Chinese-flagged ships go along. Taiwanese-owned ships become the targets.

CSIS calls this a quarantine, not a blockade. A blockade is military. A quarantine is run by police and the coast guard. And in the study's words, it "is not a declaration of war against Taiwan." Days two and

3:50 Days 2–3: the boardings

three. The boardings start. CSIS sketches two versions. In the smaller one, the pressure lands on one port.

Kaohsiung, in the south. More than ten patrol ships. About twenty militia fishing boats. Five naval groups wait over the horizon, just in case.

In the larger one, a twenty-four nautical mile zone circles the whole island. That's about twenty-eight miles. At least one or two boardings happen every day, for more than two weeks. And here's the line that matters most.

"Compliance by around 75 of shipping companies would be a considerable success for China." Read that again. Beijing doesn't need to stop every ship. It needs most shipping companies to decide the paperwork is easier than the risk. And if they do, what does Washington do?

War games, not guesswork. That's what this channel reads for you. Subscribing helps us keep reading them.

4:53 Day 4: Washington's problem

Day four. Washington's problem. The ships stopping freighters are white-hulled coast guard ships. To push back, America would send gray-hulled warships.

The CSIS authors expect U.S. forces to be cautious about exactly that. Warships against police boats is a picture nobody wants to start. And as an earlier video on this channel showed, a U.S. carrier isn't always nearby when it matters. Meanwhile, the shipping lanes through the Taiwan Strait itself stay open.

That removes one reason other countries might step in. CSIS asked experts if America would intervene militarily. Only thirteen percent of U.S. experts were "completely confident." Among Taiwan experts, it was nine percent.

5:46 Day 5: Taiwan's energy clock

Day five. Taiwan's energy clock. Taiwan is an island. Its fuel arrives by ship.

Its Ministry of Economic Affairs says natural gas stocks cover about ten to eleven days. Coal covers about thirty-nine. Oil, about a hundred forty-six. That doesn't mean the lights go out on day eleven.

A viral claim said exactly that, and Taiwan's government called false. But in CSIS's blockade wargame, the gap is stark. Natural gas runs out first. In most of the games, within about ten days.

With no preparation, Taiwan's electricity falls to about twenty percent within about two months. With preparation, it holds at about eighty. For Taiwan's twenty-three million people, that's the gap between rationing and long blackouts.

6:40 Day 6: Japan

Day six. Everyone looks at Japan. The CSIS team calls Japan "critical." Cargo can move through Japan's southwest islands toward Taiwan. And it moves under Japanese protection.

Japan also hosts the American bases any rescue would need. so a So a blockade of Taiwan quickly becomes a question for Tokyo too.

7:05 Day 7: who fires first?

Day seven. The moment the whole scenario turns on. Taiwan has three choices. Comply.

Wait. Or run by the blockade. One of the study's authors, Eric Heginbotham, put the trap in one line. A blockade "puts the onus of firing the first shot onto the Taiwanese." Another expert at the launch disagreed.

If Taiwan "decides to run the blockade," she said, "China is the one that has to escalate." So whoever moves first takes the blame. And running a blockade isn't simple either. A target to start me ago at the same event, Scott Swift, doubted the classic answer. "Convoys was a specific World War II tactic based on submarine warfare," he said.

He argued for spreading ships out instead. That's why this kind of pressure can drag on for weeks.

8:02 If someone fires: the wargames

But what if someone does fire? CSIS played that out more than twenty times. Each game ran twenty weeks, one week per turn. Their conclusion: "there are escalatory pressures at every level." Mark Cancian, who led the games, said both sides fought "with one arm tied behind their backs." Some teams found a way out.

Others "went to a high level of violence." In the worst rounds, Taiwan and the U.S. lost "over a hundred merchant ships." Both sides lost hundreds of aircraft and dozens of warships. CSIS's verdict: a blockade is not a low-risk, low-cost option for Beijing. Their earlier study, from 2023, wargamed a full invasion. In most of its twenty-four rounds, the invasion failed.

But the cost to the U.S., Taiwan and Japan was enormous. So neither path is cheap for anyone.

9:01 What it means for you

Which brings it home. What does a blockade of Taiwan mean for you? Taiwan makes about nine in ten of the world's most advanced chips. That's as of 2026, from industry estimates.

EE Times Asia puts it at ninety-two percent for the very smallest chips. They're in the phone in your pocket and the car in your driveway. And in the servers behind every AI tool. A quarantine wouldn't have to touch a single chip plant.

It would only have to slow the ships and the fuel. For Japan, Korea and Australia, it's the shipping lanes next door. For an American family, it's the price of anything with a chip inside.

9:46 Three ways it ends

So where could this go? Three ways a quarantine could end. This is analysis, not a forecast. One.

The quiet squeeze Most shipping companies file the paperwork. Remember the CSIS line. Seventy-five percent compliance would count as success. No shot is fired.

And Beijing has changed the rules around Taiwan without a war. Two. Taiwan and its partners run the blockade. Escorted cargo moves, maybe through Japan.

Then China has to choose. Back down, or shoot. That's where the wargames turned bloody. Three.

An off-ramp. Some CSIS teams found one. A quarantine lasts a week or two. Then a drawdown.

But China's ships stay closer than before. That's actually the smaller scenario CSIS described. Pressure that wins something, and stops short of war.

10:46 My verdict

My verdict: China's most likely first move isn't an invasion. It's a paperwork war at sea. It's built so that giving in looks easier than fighting back. And the hardest decision in it belongs to whoever has to fire first.
